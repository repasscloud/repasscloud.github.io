## 3. Comment replies on other people's posts

**Use from: Thursday 29 October 2026.** If you use one before then, change "out now" to "out 29 October".

Use one only when the post is actually about the topic. Add something specific to their point first, and keep the link to one line at the end. Each is under LinkedIn's 1,250-character comment limit.

### AI agents and security

---

We spent years building least-privilege access for people, then hand agents broad access because the demo looked good. An agent is still software making predictions. It doesn't know when it's wrong, so its permissions should assume it will be sometimes. I wrote about this kind of blind trust in AI for Normal People, out now: https://how-to-use-ai.com

---

### AI transformation over broken processes

---

Automating a bad process just gets you the wrong result faster. The first question shouldn't be "where can we use AI?" but "do we understand this process and what AI would actually be doing in it?" That literacy gap is why I wrote AI for Normal People, out now: https://how-to-use-ai.com

---

### AI governance and human oversight

---

Governance only works if the people doing the overseeing understand what they're overseeing. You can't evaluate an AI system's limits if nobody in the room knows it's predicting rather than reasoning. I wrote AI for Normal People for exactly those decision-makers, out now: https://how-to-use-ai.com

---

### AI-generated software

---

Generating code and engineering software are different jobs. The output can look finished while missing everything that makes it secure, maintainable and supportable. Worth remembering that the model is predicting plausible code, not checking it. More on how these tools work in AI for Normal People: https://how-to-use-ai.com

---

### RAG, accuracy and hallucinations

---

RAG improves the odds, but the model is still generating text from patterns, so it can still be fluently wrong, even with the right document in front of it. The confidence of the answer tells you nothing about its accuracy. I explain why AI sounds so sure of itself in AI for Normal People: https://how-to-use-ai.com

---

### Agentic products and user control

---

Once software starts taking initiative, the questions change: who's accountable, what can the user see, and how do they stop it? Those are easier to answer when users understand what the system is actually doing. That's the case I make in AI for Normal People, out now: https://how-to-use-ai.com

---

### Data and "insights"

---

Finding a pattern isn't the same as understanding it. AI is very good at the first and has no access to the second, so someone still has to ask whether the answer makes sense. That distinction is at the heart of AI for Normal People, out now: https://how-to-use-ai.com

---

### When someone asks "who's the book for?"

---

Anyone who uses AI, approves it or is affected by it, which is most of us now. It's written for non-technical readers: no maths, no code, no jargon without an explanation. Managers and decision-makers get the most out of it, because they're the ones being asked to sign off on AI without a clear picture of what it is. https://how-to-use-ai.com
